# Writing answers that score

Evaluators often score 20–100 proposals against a sheet, under time pressure. Write for that reader: make the answer easy to find, easy to score and easy to believe.

## Answer structure (for each requirement)

1. **Direct answer first.** Start with "Yes, …", "We will …" or the direct answer, using the RFP's own keywords. An evaluator skimming for "do they comply?" should find it in the first sentence.
2. **How.** Give the approach, process or tool, specifically: named steps, roles and frequencies.
3. **Proof.** Cite evidence from the library: a certification, a case study with numbers, or a named method. Prefer one strong, specific proof over three vague ones.
4. **So what for this client.** Link to a goal or constraint stated in the RFP: "…which supports your goal of reducing call-centre volume."

Keep answers proportional. A yes/no compliance item needs 1–3 sentences. A weighted "describe your approach" question deserves the full structure, within the word limit.

## Tailoring checklist

- Use the client's name and their terminology (their name for the system, their departments, their goals).
- Reflect the evaluation criteria. Spend words where the points are.
- Mirror the RFP's structure and numbering in the response, so evaluators don't have to search.
- Executive summary: their challenge → our solution → why us (win themes) → proof → the outcome they get. Keep it to one page. It's written for the decision-maker, not the technical evaluator.

## Win themes

A win theme is a client benefit backed by a differentiator and proof. For example: "Lower risk at go-live: our phased rollout has delivered 6 public portals with zero critical incidents at launch (CS-002, CS-005)." Pick 2–4 themes that match the evaluation criteria and repeat them in the executive summary, the approach and the closing.

## Style

- Active voice, "we" and "you". Short sentences. No filler ("best-in-class", "cutting-edge", "world-class", "synergy", "leverage"), because evaluators discount it.
- Specific beats general: "two-week sprints with a demo to your product owner" beats "agile methodology".
- Use tables for comparisons, plans and team composition. Use bullets only for genuinely parallel items.
- Be consistent: use the same names for products, roles and phases throughout.

## Never

- Invent or "round up" facts: certifications, client names, figures, team members, dates, prices.
- Promise more than the library or the user supports. Use `[VERIFY: …]` instead.
- Leave another client's name or details from reused text.
- Hide a non-compliance. State it as an exception with an alternative.
